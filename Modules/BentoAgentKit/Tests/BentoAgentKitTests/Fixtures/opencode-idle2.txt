     A Brief History of Terminal Multiplexers                                                                           
                                                                                                                        
     The story of terminal multiplexers begins in the early days of Unix, when system administrators and developers     
     needed ways to manage multiple programs from a single physical terminal. Before multiplexers existed, users were   
     limited to one foreground process per terminal connection, a constraint that became increasingly frustrating as    
     computing tasks grew more complex and interdependent.                                                              
                                                                                                                        
     The earliest influential solution was screen, created in 1987 by Oliver Laumann at the University of Karlsruhe.    
     Screen introduced the revolutionary concept of virtual terminals: multiple shell sessions running within a         
     single physical terminal, switchable with simple keyboard shortcuts. Equally important, screen allowed sessions    
     to detach—continuing to run in the background even after the user disconnected. This was transformative for        
     unreliable network connections over dial-up or SSH, where a dropped link no longer meant a killed process.         
     Screen quickly became indispensable in the Unix toolkit and remained dominant for over two decades.                
                                                                                                                        
     In 2007, tmux emerged, written by Nicholas Marriott as a modern alternative. Tmux addressed long-standing          
     frustrations with screen's aging codebase and limited extensibility. It offered a cleaner client-server            
     architecture, powerful scripting capabilities, and sophisticated window splitting through panes. Its               
     configuration syntax was more approachable, and active development attracted a vibrant community. By the 2010s,    
     tmux had largely supplanted screen as the multiplexer of choice for new users.                                     
                                                                                                                        
     Beyond screen and tmux, related tools expanded the concept. GNU Screen received ongoing maintenance, while         
     projects like byobu wrapped tmux and screen with friendlier defaults and status indicators. More recently,         
     terminal emulators such as iTerm2, Kitty, and Alacritty, alongside protocol innovations like iTerm2's tmux         
     integration and Zellij—a Rust-based newcomer—have pushed multiplexing forward with tiling layouts, persistent      
     layouts, and modern ergonomics.                                                                                    
                                                                                                                        
     Terminal multiplexers remain essential today, bridging the gap between minimalist text interfaces and the          
     demands of complex, multi-process workflows. Their evolution reflects a simple truth: developers will always       
     seek finer control over their computing environment.
